# Gordon Watts: voice and slide content

Speak directly and conversationally about concrete scientific work. Use technical terminology when the selected audience understands it; define new concepts and unfamiliar jargon. Write plain short sentences and fragments that support a spoken explanation rather than a standalone essay. Avoid marketing language, slogans and invented personal anecdotes. Use first person for actual experience and "we" for shared scientific problems.

Natural contractions, parentheses, candid caveats and occasional informal or self-deprecating humor fit this voice. Use emphatic remarks sparingly when there is a real point. Correct accidental spelling and grammar rather than preserving a historical mistake as style. Distinguish measured observation, demonstration, interpretation and speculation. Keep important qualifications next to the result.

## Titles and density

Use plain topic labels, genuine questions, or a supported takeaway as the content warrants. Do not force every title into a claim or slogan. Capitalize important words in new slide titles; keep minor words such as "the", "of", "and", and "its" lowercase unless first. Preserve scientific symbols, acronyms and proper names. Ellipses can express a transition or unfinished spoken thought where natural.

Vary density deliberately while retaining consistent visual conventions. For a typical evidence slide, start with one main visual and roughly 25–60 words of commentary. This is a starting point, not a limit. Complex graphics warrant less prose. An exact prompt, quotation, reproducible task or summary may justify more. Quiet question and section slides can be almost empty. Do not impose a three-bullet rule or make dense conclusions the default.

Use short bullets, with one nested level where qualifications need it. Keep exact prompts and recorded results intact when they are evidence; put long explanations in notes. Do not require the audience to read an entire paper screenshot to follow the argument.

## Narrative and repeated patterns

Start with a question the audience cares about, define enough to understand the demonstration, show concrete evidence, explain success and limitation, and broaden to scientific implications. Use a roadmap or quiet section change when it helps the audience change scale. This pattern does not prescribe a subject sequence.

An overview can precede enlarged details. Related slides can preserve the same title, prompt or graphic while developing the interpretation. Repeated diagrams and progressive reveals are useful when they maintain orientation. A plot plus commentary should say what the result demonstrates and what it cannot establish. Paper discussions separate the authors' words from presenter interpretation. Backup material should deepen questions raised in the main talk.

Occasional metaphor or humorous artwork is appropriate; scientific evidence remains central. An extravagant title image, dashboard-like imported slide or dense mind map from an earlier talk is not a default style requirement. Use [visual guidance](visual.md) only when choosing layouts or formatting.
